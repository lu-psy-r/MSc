Critical citation practice

Written by Rob Davies

NoteOverview

In this lecture, we focus on critical citation practice.

We look at:

  1. Overview: what we are doing when we cite scholarly work.
  2. What are the different reasons for citing work? How can we misuse citations?
  3. Science as a credit economy: who gets the credit? Authorship, contributorship, and the ethics of identifying who does what.
  4. Revisiting being and doing science: a critical analysis of peer review, critical discourse, and the response to error.
  5. Is science self-correcting? Mechanisms or pathways for science to correct itself. How should we respond to error?
  6. What analysis of citations shows us about inequities and biases in science.

We do this so we can figure out our answers to these questions:

  • How do we proceed in a manner that is reflective or self-aware in ways that are useful or helpful to ourselves and others?
  • How do we break free of the biases or inequities or limitations that citation analyses appear to reveal?

Next, you can see the lecture elements. We recommend that you watch them in sequence.

  1. Overview (~7 minutes). What we are going to do, the core questions: what we are doing when we cite scholarly work, who work gets into the public domain, what happens if we find errors in the work, how we use citations to evaluate science and scientists, and what that reveals about inequities or biases.

  1. The different reasons for citing work (~12 minutes). What are the different reasons for citing work? How can we misuse citations?

  1. Science as a credit economy (~15 minutes). Why do people operate in the way that they do when we look at citation practice? Science as a credit economy, who gets the credit, authorship, contributorship, and the ethics of identifying who does what.

  1. Revisiting being and doing science (~25 minutes). In order to think clearly about what work we cite, and why, we need to revisit our understanding of how the work gets into the public domain so that we can cite it. Here, we need to deepen and reflect critically on processes of peer review or critical discourse before and after publication.

  1. Is science self-correcting? (~32 minutes). What happens when we find errors in work that is in the public domain? Is science self-correcting? Mechanisms or pathways for science to correct itself. The ways in which researchers have or have not responded to the identification of errors in work, in their citation behaviors.

  1. What analysis of citations shows us (~10 minutes). What analyses of citations show about inequity and bias in who does science, and in whose science has influence.
